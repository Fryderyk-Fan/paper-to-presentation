# Slide design and evidence

## Choose a route that fits the contribution

Start with what the audience needs to understand the argument. Combine or expand the following stages according to the paper and the intended audience.

| Contribution | Possible route | Evidence worth showing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Algorithm or formal method | Define the task and assumptions → show the mechanism → work through an example → explain the guarantee or evaluation | Core update rule or invariant, cost or trade-off, comparison with a relevant baseline |
| Experimental or observational study | Frame the question → explain the design → show the main finding → interpret its scope | Sample and conditions, comparison, effect size or uncertainty, limitations on generalization or causation |
| System or applied case | Introduce the problem in context → explain the workflow → walk through a case → show the supported result | System diagram, real case, representative output, reported evaluation |
| Dataset, benchmark, or taxonomy | Explain the need → show how it was built → explain coverage and use → identify scope limits | Data sources, construction rules, coverage, metric definitions, one example |
| Review, synthesis, or conceptual paper | Define the scope → explain the selection or organizing framework → compare themes or positions → give the supported implications | Inclusion criteria when reported, comparison matrix, taxonomy, patterns and exceptions |

Use these routes as examples. Give each slide a specific role in the paper's argument. An essential method step, experiment, or interpretation may need its own slide. Show an algorithm's essential inputs, outputs, and decisions. Use a small, faithfully worked example or the most informative equation when it helps the audience follow the mechanism. Leave full derivations and long tables in the paper unless their detail is essential to the requested explanation.

## Pair each slide with evidence and narration

- Build each slide around one main message from the shared outline. Put the relevant visual evidence, structure, and necessary academic context on the slide. Use the narration to explain how to read it and why it matters. Write labels and supporting text for the visual. Avoid copying full narration sentences or closely paraphrasing them into titles and bullets.
- Trace every factual claim to the supplied PDF. Prefer the precise wording and conditions in the methods, results, figures, and discussion over broad wording in the abstract. Check numbers against their tables or figures. Use precise academic language and avoid promotional claims about novelty, performance, impact, or causality. Include results, demonstrated benefits, or applications only when the paper supports them. Handle author proposals, illustrative cases, and reported findings according to what the paper actually shows.
- For evaluations, retain important denominators, units, conditions, baselines, and uncertainty. Match the strength of the claim to the study design. Use exact values when they matter to the comparison or interpretation. Use a concise qualitative summary when it preserves the conclusion and its scope. For papers without a reported evaluation, explain the supported contribution, such as a theorem with its assumptions, a dataset's coverage, or a framework's organizing rationale.

## Use figures and typography for a live explanation

- Use the selected delivery language for slide titles, body text, captions, callouts, and labels in newly created visuals. Preserve technical acronyms, symbols, and labels embedded in original paper figures. Use an external label or the narration to explain important original labels in the selected language.
- Use a concise academic title and a clearly ordered composition. For text-led slides, start with one short statement or two to four short bullets. Use layouts that suit the evidence, such as a full figure with external callouts, a pipeline, a comparison, or a worked example. Keep typography, alignment, spacing, and color consistent. Avoid dense paragraphs, long copied passages, and decoration that competes with the evidence. Use safe margins and readable text. Titles around 28 pt or above and body text around 18 pt or above work as starting points on widescreen slides. Inspect Chinese glyphs and line breaks when using Chinese text.
- Use compact evidence references close to the claim or visual. Use printed page numbers when unambiguous, and PDF page numbers with a `PDF p.` label when necessary. Identify the paper by title and enough citation information to find it. Use separate credits or references only when they add value within the page limit.

## Preserve original figures

1. Prefer a complete, relevant paper figure that is legible at presentation size. Extract the embedded original if it represents the complete figure. For vector figures or composites assembled from several elements in the PDF, render the complete figure faithfully from the source page. Isolate its outer boundary while retaining all panels, axes, legends, annotations, and labels. Use a brief source label such as `Fig. 3, p. 6` with a concise explanation instead of the full published caption.
2. Use proportional contain or fit. Keep all figure edges inside the slide. Preserve colors, proportions, panel order, and internal content. Never crop to a panel, fill by cropping, stretch, mask, retouch, recolor, or redraw an image presented as the original. Keep slide labels and callouts outside the figure.
3. If the full figure is illegible, try a simpler complete source figure for the same point. When appropriate, create a clearly labeled explanatory schematic based on the paper, or chart its reported data while retaining relevant units and scales. Credit the source and make clear that the visual was created for the talk. Omit the visual if a faithful substitute would require guessing.

## Inspect the exported deck

- Review every exported slide at presentation size. Check the slide against its narration and the paper. Confirm that the main message, figure or evidence, and spoken explanation agree. Check that each original figure matches the PDF and remains complete and readable, including its labels and legend.
- Check equations, symbols, text and object bounds, margins, and overlap. Look for clipping, hidden overflow, and off-slide objects.
- Keep the finished file limited to the research content and essential presentation information. Remove unused placeholders, hidden drafts, and generation details such as prompt text, internal notes, intermediate file names, local paths, commands, and logs. Check slide and chart text, speaker notes, comments, links, alt text, document properties, and hidden objects.
